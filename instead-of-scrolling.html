Something better to do than scrolling

Blocking apps solves the wrong half of the problem. The hard part was never the app. It was not knowing what else to do with the next ten minutes.

Why blockers stop working

Most people who try to use their phone less have already tried a blocker. App timers, focus modes, a grayscale filter, a tree that dies if you leave. They work, for a few days, and then they come off.

The usual explanation is willpower, which is both unkind and wrong. Look at what actually happens in the moment a blocker fires. You reach for the phone, something stops you, and you put it down. You are now standing in a kitchen with nine minutes and forty seconds of nothing. Nobody told you what to do with it. So you unlock the phone again, tap through the warning, and the blocker has taught you only that it can be tapped through.

Blocking is good at the first half of the problem — interrupting the reach. It does nothing at all about the second half, which is the part that decides whether you reach again. The gap does not get filled by being told off.

The problem was never the app. It was the empty ten minutes.

What works instead: one specific small thing

The thing that reliably works is unglamorous. Have one specific small thing in mind, sized to the time that is actually in front of you.

Not a plan for the day, and not a list. One thing. The specificity is the whole mechanism — "be more active" loses to "walk to the end of the road and back" every time, because the second one tells you what to do with your feet in the next four seconds. And the sizing matters as much: an idea that needs twenty-five minutes is useless when you have eight, so it gets refused, and refusing twice is how people stop asking.

Ten minutes

  • Walk to the end of the street and back. No podcast, no target.
  • Message one person a real sentence — not a reaction, something you actually thought.
  • Ten pages of whatever is nearest.
  • Stretch, badly, on the floor.
  • Make the drink you keep meaning to make properly.
  • Write down the three things on your mind so they stop circling.

Two minutes

  • Stand up and look at something more than six metres away.
  • Fill the glass of water you have been not filling.
  • Open the window.
  • Put four things back where they live.
  • Breathe out for longer than you breathe in, eight times.

An hour that opened up

  • Go to the one place nearby you have never actually been into.
  • Cook something that takes attention.
  • Call the person you keep meaning to call.
  • Take the long way somewhere you were going anyway.

None of this needs an app. If you read this page, close it, and go stand outside for ten minutes, the page has done its job.

Why "not today" has to be a real answer

Any system that offers you something has to let you decline without cost. The moment declining produces a broken streak, a lost tree, a red mark or a guilt-shaped notification, two things happen: you start declining dishonestly, and then you stop opening the thing at all.

So the decline has to be free, and it has to be free permanently — not "free this once". Rest is a legitimate answer. So is entertainment. So is doing nothing on purpose. A system that cannot say that is not helping you build a life; it is running a scoreboard with a kinder font.

Where Chrysa fits

Chrysa is the second half of the problem, built into the moment the first half happens.

You pick the apps you open on autopilot. When one of them opens, Chrysa offers one small thing sized to the time you actually have, drawn from what it already knows about your day — how you slept, whether you have walked, how long you have been on the phone, what is left of your evening. You can open the app anyway; nothing is blocked, and it does not ask why. "Not today" is a complete answer, and it never costs anything.

What you do instead becomes the land outside a window on your home screen: a tree for a walk, a lit window for an evening with someone, a bird for twenty pages. There is no score, nothing to keep up, and nothing out there decays if you stop.

It is free, it has no account, and everything stays on your phone.
